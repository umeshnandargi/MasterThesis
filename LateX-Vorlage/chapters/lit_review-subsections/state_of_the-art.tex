\section{State of the Art}
\label{sec:state_of_the_art}

\subsection{Grasp Planning for a Fixed End-Effector}
Most work on robotic grasping assumes that the gripper is given and searches for a suitable grasp on the workpiece\cite{Zurbruegg2025GraspQP}.
Analytical methods evaluate candidate contacts through force- and form-closure conditions, while data-driven methods learn a grasp quality predictor
from example data. In both cases the decision variable is the grasp pose, and the geometry of the gripper is a fixed input. This limits the achievable
result\cite{Zurbruegg2025GraspQP}. If the suction cup arrangement or the jaw contour does not match the parts that have to be handled, no grasp
planner can recover the stability or the feasible grasp positions that are lost. The geometry of the gripper is therefore a design variable that is
rarely used\cite{Todescato}.

\subsection{Multi-Suction Grasping}
Work on suction-based handling has focused on operation rather than design. Model-free strategies decide whether a suction contact will hold without
an explicit analytical seal model \cite{Schillinger2023Model-Free}. Experimental studies measure how surface properties and local curvature affect the
achievable holding force and the reliability of individual cups \cite{Geng2025Experimental}. Other work selects grasp poses, activates suitable
subsets of cups, and plans motions that stay within the resulting load limits \cite{Lee2024Grasp}. In all of these contributions the spatial
arrangement of the cups is taken from an existing array and is not optimized. The arrangement, however, controls an important trade-off. A widely
distributed layout increases the resistible moment and therefore the stability of the grasp, but it also reduces the number of workpiece regions on
which all cups can be sealed at the same time, and therefore the grasp availability. None of the cited works formulates this trade-off as a design
problem.

\subsection{Two-Finger Grasping and Jaw Geometry}
For form- and friction-closure grippers, existing work improves grasp robustness mainly through mechanical means. Compliant, soft and underactuated
finger structures adapt passively to unknown contours and tolerate uncertainty in object pose and shape \cite{Sun2022LARG:, Xie2024Design}. Geometric
methods reason about contact regions and finger placement derived from the object model \cite{Dong2018Geometric}. Direct optimization of rigid jaw
geometry for a specific set of parts has received less attention, although custom machined jaws are common in industrial bin picking and assembly. In
particular, the jaw contour is seldom treated as a continuous design variable that is evaluated against workpiece-dependent and collision-aware
criteria. It is usually derived from the nominal part outline by simple offsetting rules.

\subsection{Research Gap and Contribution}
Current research has made substantial progress in robotic grasp planning, adaptive gripper design, and task-specific end-effector optimization. In
multi-suction grasping, recent methods focus on grasp pose selection, cup activation strategies, and motion safety, but typically assume a fixed
suction layout and do not systematically study how cup arrangement affects the trade-off between stability and grasp availability
\cite{Schillinger2023Model-Free, Geng2025Experimental, Lee2024Grasp}. In two-finger grasping, prior work has addressed compliant, soft, and
underactuated finger designs, but direct optimization of rigid jaw geometry using workpiece-dependent, collision-aware metrics remains less explored
\cite{Sun2022LARG:, Dong2018Geometric, Xie2024Design}. Furthermore, none of these works conduct a comparative evaluation of optimization algorithms
for the respective problem class, and their optimization and evaluation components are not separated in a way that would permit one.

This thesis addresses these gaps. First, gripper geometry is treated as the primary optimization variable for both end-effector types,
and the geometric evaluation is formulated as a black-box multi-objective problem over a diverse set of workpieces. Second, a modular
optimization--evaluation framework is developed in which the expensive geometric evaluation is exposed behind a common interface and the optimizer is
interchangeable, so that any algorithm operates on an identical objective definition. This separation is used to benchmark evolutionary and
Bayesian multi-objective algorithms on both problems under a shared evaluation budget, with standard quality indicators, repeated runs and
statistical tests. The contribution therefore lies not only in the problem formulations, but in the framework that makes a controlled comparison
possible and in the comparison itself.
